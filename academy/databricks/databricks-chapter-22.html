Databricks Cost Optimization

Understand what you pay for, measure it from Databricks' own billing tables, attribute it to teams and workloads, and cut waste without breaking SLAs. Cost optimization isn't "use a smaller cluster": it's delivering the required processing within the SLA, reliably, at the lowest practical total cost.

The situation this chapter fixes

Fifty production jobs: some run five minutes, some two hours. A few interactive clusters sit idle overnight. Several SQL queries scan far more than they need. Without cost monitoring you pay for oversized compute, inefficient queries, repeated processing, and forgotten resources, and nobody can say which team or job is responsible.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  1. Explain DBUs and the components of Databricks cost.
  2. Compare serverless and classic compute.
  3. Optimize jobs, clusters, and SQL queries.
  4. Price usage and attribute it to jobs and teams.
  5. Find expensive and wasteful workloads.
  6. Build cost dashboards, budgets, and alerts.
  7. Recommend improvements backed by measurements.
What was tested

Every cost query in 22.3 to 22.5 was run on Spark 4 against practice tables that use the real system-table column names (the generator is in 22.5, so you can run the same thing on Free Edition). The outputs shown are real results from that invented data. All prices and amounts in this chapter are illustrative, not actual Azure Databricks prices.

22.1.2What Is a DBU?

A DBU (Databricks Unit) is a normalized unit of Databricks platform consumption. It's not a VM hour: two one-hour workloads can consume different DBUs depending on node types, node count, Photon, and product (jobs, all-purpose, SQL, serverless).

Worked example (illustrative)

A job consumes 8 DBUs per hour and runs 2 hours:

DBUs = 8 × 2 = 16 DBUs

At an illustrative ₹25 per DBU:

Platform cost = 16 × ₹25 = ₹400, plus, on classic compute, the VMs underneath.

MetricMeaning
DBU consumptionQuantity of platform usage
DBU ratePrice per DBU for that SKU
Cloud infrastructureVMs, disks (classic compute)
Total workload costPlatform + infrastructure + storage + network + services
Rate matters as much as quantity

The same workload costs different amounts on different SKUs. All-purpose (interactive) compute has a higher DBU rate than jobs compute, so a scheduled job on an interactive cluster pays more for identical work (22.5 finds exactly this). And fewer DBUs isn't automatically cheaper overall: a bigger cluster that finishes far sooner can cost less in total. Measure.

Why it matters

Here the VMs cost more than the DBUs, so optimizing DBUs alone misses most of the bill.

  • Less idle time cuts DBUs and VM hours.
  • Faster queries cut runtime.
  • Fewer intermediate copies cut storage and network.
  • A different compute model changes the cost structure.

Classic clusters' VMs live in the workspace's managed resource group and carry Databricks' default tags plus your custom tags, which is how Azure Cost Management can split them by team (22.3.5).

22.1.4Serverless Pricing Concepts

With serverless, Databricks runs the compute: fast startup, automatic scaling, no clusters to configure, and one DBU rate that already includes the infrastructure (no separate VM line). Availability varies by region, workspace, and workload type.

FactorServerlessClassic compute
InfrastructureManaged by DatabricksYou configure node types, sizes, pools
StartupSeconds, typicallyMinutes (VM provisioning)
Idle costLittle or none between runsPaid until termination
Configuration controlLimited (no init scripts, fixed runtime)Full control

Is serverless always cheaper? No.

A job that runs five minutes every hour on an always-on classic cluster pays for 55 idle minutes an hour; serverless avoids that. But a long, steady, well-sized batch job may be cheaper on classic job compute, and some workloads need configurations serverless doesn't offer. Compare measured cost per successful run for both before deciding. Docs: Serverless compute.

22.1.5SQL Warehouse Costs

Driven by size, running time, scaling (min/max clusters), concurrency, idle time, type (serverless, pro, classic), and query efficiency. A dashboard warehouse left on all day for users who only work business hours pays for every idle hour.

Levers

  • Auto-stop: serverless warehouses start in seconds, so a short auto-stop (a few minutes) costs users little.
  • Right-size using query history (queued vs running time).
  • Scaling: raise max clusters for concurrency, not size.
  • Optimize expensive queries (22.2.6).
  • Fewer refreshes: don't refresh dashboards more often than data changes.

Don't just pick the smallest

An undersized warehouse runs queries longer and queues users. A larger one can cost less if it finishes enough faster, since you pay per second while it's running. Balance cost, latency, and concurrency, measured from system.query.history (Chapter 20). Docs: SQL warehouses.

22.1.6Storage, Network, and Data Transfer

Storage

Raw files, Bronze/Silver/Gold tables, temporary files, checkpoints, old Delta versions, logs, exports. A 100 GB daily intermediate kept for a year is 100 × 365 = 36,500 GB (≈36.5 TB) of logical data, before compression and redundancy.

  • Don't persist unneeded intermediates.
  • Set retention; VACUUM removes unreferenced files after the retention period.
  • Compact small files when it measurably helps.
  • Cool or archive tiers for rarely read raw data.

Network

Cross-region reads and writes, exports, on-premises transfers, external APIs, and repeated copies between environments. Reading 2 TB from one region and writing it to another daily adds transfer charges and time, however efficient the Spark code.

  • Process data in its own region.
  • Avoid repeated full copies; share instead (Delta Sharing, Chapter 23).
  • Filter before exporting.
  • Question whether cross-region design is needed.
Never delete Delta files by hand

Removing files from storage directly corrupts tables. Use VACUUM (and understand it ends time travel to older versions, Chapter 6) or let predictive optimization manage it for Unity Catalog managed tables. Docs: VACUUM.

The optimization loop

  1. Establish a baseline.
  2. Measure runtime and resource use.
  3. Find the bottleneck (Chapter 20).
  4. Test one change.
  5. Measure again.
  6. Compare cost and performance.
  7. Keep the cheapest config that meets the SLA.
Cost per successful run

Total workload cost ÷ successful runs. ₹1,200 over 10 successful runs = ₹120 per run. The total must include failed attempts and retries; they consumed compute but delivered nothing, which is exactly why they belong in the numerator. 22.3.4 computes this from real billing data.

Align schedules with when source data actually arrives, use incremental processing, remove duplicate schedules, prevent pointless overlaps (max concurrent runs = 1), run heavy jobs in suitable windows, use event-driven triggers, and consolidate compatible small jobs into one multi-task job so they share compute.

Switching full refresh to incremental is only safe once you've handled change detection, deletes, late-arriving data, and recovery (Chapters 12–13).

22.2.3Automatic Termination and Autoscaling

Auto-termination

Stops an all-purpose cluster after N idle minutes. Finish at 4:00 PM with a 30-minute setting, and it stops at 4:30 instead of running all night.

Use it on development, interactive analysis, and ad hoc clusters; enforce it with a compute policy (Chapter 19).

Job clusters already end when the job ends; the fix there is making sure jobs use job or serverless compute rather than a shared interactive cluster.

Autoscaling

Adds and removes workers between a minimum and maximum (e.g. 2–8) as demand changes.

  • Keep minimums low for spiky jobs.
  • Check whether the job actually scales; a skewed stage won't use extra workers (Chapter 20).
  • Startup and scale-up take time, which matters for short jobs.
  • Test with representative data.

Autoscaling doesn't fix an inefficient job; it just runs it on more machines.

22.2.5Query Optimization

Wasted compute comes from reading unneeded columns and rows, repeated full-table aggregations, inefficient joins, recomputing the same results, large shuffles, too many small files, and poor data layout. Chapter 15 covers the techniques; here's how to check what Spark actually reads.

Python · filter and select early, then confirm in the plan
from pyspark.sql import functions as F

orders = (spark.read.table("retail_dev.bronze.orders")
          .filter(F.col("order_status") == "COMPLETED")
          .select("order_id", "customer_id", "order_date", "amount"))

orders.explain("formatted")     # look at the Scan node
Output (verified: 200,000-row Parquet table with a wide notes column)
PushedFilters: [IsNotNull(order_status), EqualTo(order_status,COMPLETED)]
ReadSchema: struct&lt;order_id:int,customer_id:string,order_date:string,
                   amount:double,order_status:string&gt;

The 200-character notes column isn't read at all (column pruning), and the filter is pushed into the scan, so files and row groups that can't match are skipped. The original selected first and filtered on a column it had dropped; Spark still resolves that, but filtering before selecting is clearer and safer. On Delta, data skipping and liquid clustering (Chapter 15) multiply the effect.

Counts cost compute

Each count() scans data. Keep counts you need for reconciliation (Chapter 18) but compute several metrics in one aggregation rather than many separate actions, and don't count "just to print".

Find the expensive queries

Rank by total_duration_ms and bytes read in system.query.history (Chapter 20), then open the query profile. A small number of queries usually account for most warehouse time.

22.2.6Avoiding Unnecessary Reruns and File Operations

Reruns that waste compute

  • Rerunning the whole pipeline after only a downstream task failed.
  • Retrying permanent data-quality failures.
  • Reprocessing completed batches.
  • Duplicate schedules.
  • Rerunning because audit data is incomplete.
  • Rebuilding all history to fix one partition.

If Extract → Bronze → Silver → Gold succeed and Report Refresh fails, use Repair run in Jobs to rerun only the failed task, provided upstream outputs are valid (idempotency, Chapter 18).

Too many small files

10,000 tiny files mean more listing, planning, metadata, task scheduling, and storage transactions.

  • Avoid over-partitioning.
  • Optimized writes and auto compaction.
  • OPTIMIZE (or predictive optimization) when it measurably helps.
  • Review streaming trigger intervals (Chapter 16).
  • Avoid repeated full overwrites.

Very large files hurt parallelism too. Measure before and after.

22.3Cost Monitoring

22.3.1Billing System Tables

Two system tables are the foundation of cost analysis (an admin enables the system.billing schema and grants access):

TableOne row perKey columns
system.billing.usageUsage record (hourly or finer)usage_date, sku_name, usage_unit, usage_quantity, custom_tags, usage_metadata (job_id, job_run_id, job_name, cluster_id, warehouse_id…), identity_metadata.run_as, billing_origin_product, product_features.is_serverless, record_type
system.billing.list_pricesSKU price changesku_name, price_start_time, price_end_time, currency_code, pricing.effective_list.default
Billing corrections: sum, don't filter

When Databricks corrects a record it adds a RETRACTION (negative quantity) and a RESTATEMENT. SUM(usage_quantity) nets them out automatically. Filtering to record_type = 'ORIGINAL' double-counts. In the practice data, one corrected product-load record makes the job total 35.64 DBUs when summed correctly versus 35.84 with the filter (verified).

Usage is not the invoice

List price × usage is an estimate. Your bill reflects negotiated discounts, pre-purchased Databricks commit units, credits, currency (list prices are in USD), and separately billed VMs, storage, and network. Label estimates clearly and reconcile totals with Azure Cost Management. Data arrives with a delay of several hours.

Before writing queries, DESCRIBE TABLE system.billing.usage in your workspace. Docs: Billable usage table · Pricing table.

22.3.2Compute Usage Analysis: Usage by SKU

SQL
-- DBUs by SKU over the last 30 days. Summing nets out billing corrections
-- (RETRACTION rows are negative), so don't filter on record_type.
SELECT sku_name,
       usage_unit,
       round(SUM(usage_quantity), 2) AS total_usage
FROM system.billing.usage
WHERE usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 30)
GROUP BY sku_name, usage_unit
ORDER BY total_usage DESC

22.3.3Pricing the Usage

The original stopped at quantities. To estimate money, join each usage record to the list price that was in effect when it ran. Prices change, so the join must respect price_start_time and price_end_time. A view keeps every later query simple:

SQL · one reusable view
CREATE SCHEMA IF NOT EXISTS retail_dev.cost_ops;

-- Each usage record priced at the list price that was in force when it ended
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW retail_dev.cost_ops.usage_list_cost AS
SELECT u.*,
       u.usage_quantity * lp.pricing.effective_list.default AS list_cost,
       lp.currency_code
FROM system.billing.usage AS u
LEFT JOIN system.billing.list_prices AS lp
  ON  lp.sku_name   = u.sku_name
  AND lp.cloud      = u.cloud
  AND lp.usage_unit = u.usage_unit
  AND u.usage_end_time >= lp.price_start_time
  AND (lp.price_end_time IS NULL OR u.usage_end_time < lp.price_end_time);

The practice data includes a mid-month price change for jobs compute, and the view prices each day correctly. Always check for usage with no matching price (Finding 4 in 22.5) before trusting totals.

22.3.4Job-Level Consumption

Usage records for jobs carry usage_metadata.job_id and job_run_id, so job cost can be computed directly and joined to run history for cost per successful run:

SQL
-- List-price cost per job, joined to run history for cost per successful run
WITH job_cost AS (
    SELECT usage_metadata.job_id                AS job_id,
           max(usage_metadata.job_name)         AS job_name,
           round(SUM(usage_quantity), 1)        AS dbus,
           round(SUM(list_cost), 2)             AS list_cost_usd,
           max(product_features.is_serverless)  AS serverless
    FROM retail_dev.cost_ops.usage_list_cost
    WHERE usage_metadata.job_id IS NOT NULL
      AND usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 30)
    GROUP BY usage_metadata.job_id
),
job_runs AS (
    SELECT job_id,
           count(DISTINCT run_id)                                              AS runs,
           count(DISTINCT CASE WHEN result_state = 'SUCCEEDED' THEN run_id END) AS successful_runs
    FROM system.lakeflow.job_run_timeline
    WHERE result_state IS NOT NULL                       -- final row of each run only
      AND period_start_time >= date_sub(current_date(), 30)
    GROUP BY job_id
)
SELECT c.job_name, c.serverless, c.dbus, c.list_cost_usd, r.runs, r.successful_runs,
       round(c.list_cost_usd / nullif(r.successful_runs, 0), 2) AS cost_per_successful_run
FROM job_cost AS c
LEFT JOIN job_runs AS r ON r.job_id = c.job_id
ORDER BY c.list_cost_usd DESC
Output (verified; USD at invented list prices)
job_name           serverless  dbus   list_cost_usd  runs  successful_runs  cost_per_successful_run
sales_aggregation  false       623.9  192.47         30    30               6.42
customer_load      true        62.1   27.93          31    30               0.93
product_load       true        35.6   16.04          30    30               0.53
Look what's missing

historical_backfill doesn't appear. It ran on a shared all-purpose cluster, so its usage is billed to that cluster, not to the job. Jobs on shared interactive clusters are invisible to job-level cost and pay the higher all-purpose rate. That's why 22.5 searches for them explicitly. When exact attribution isn't possible, label the result an estimate and document the allocation method.

customer_load had 31 runs but 30 successes: the failed attempt's cost is included, raising its cost per successful run, as it should.

-- Chargeback view: cost per cost centre, with untagged spend made visible

22.3.7Identifying Expensive Workloads

Long runtime alone doesn't make a workload "expensive"; weigh cost against business value. Useful indicators:

  • Total consumption and cost.
  • Cost per successful run.
  • Cost per million records or per TB.
  • Runtime vs baseline (Chapter 20).
  • Failed-run and retry cost.
  • Idle cost.
  • SLA compliance.

Cost per million records

(Total cost ÷ records) × 1,000,000

₹600 to process 5 million records → (600 ÷ 5,000,000) × 1,000,000 = ₹120 per million.

Only compare workloads of similar complexity; record counts come from Chapter 20's audit table.

22.5.1Part A: The Sample Cost Table

SQL · steps 1–5 (hypothetical estimates, not billing data)
CREATE SCHEMA IF NOT EXISTS retail_dev.cost_ops;

CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS retail_dev.cost_ops.job_cost_sample (
    job_name STRING, environment STRING, execution_count INT,
    average_duration_minutes DOUBLE, estimated_cost_per_run DOUBLE, monthly_cost DOUBLE
);

INSERT INTO retail_dev.cost_ops.job_cost_sample VALUES
    ('customer_load',       'PROD', 30,  12,  120,  3600),
    ('product_load',        'PROD', 30,   8,   75,  2250),
    ('sales_aggregation',   'PROD', 30,  45,  480, 14400),
    ('historical_backfill', 'PROD',  2, 240, 3500,  7000);

-- Most expensive jobs, cost per run, cost per minute, share of total
SELECT job_name,
       monthly_cost,
       round(monthly_cost / execution_count, 2)                                    AS cost_per_execution,
       round(monthly_cost / nullif(execution_count * average_duration_minutes, 0), 2) AS cost_per_minute,
       round(100 * monthly_cost / SUM(monthly_cost) OVER (), 1)                    AS pct_of_total
FROM retail_dev.cost_ops.job_cost_sample
ORDER BY monthly_cost DESC;

Total monthly estimated cost: 27,250

Sales aggregation is the largest monthly cost (53%); the backfill is the dearest per run and per minute, which may be justified for a necessary activity, but worth checking. Cost per minute is a rough ratio; compare similar workloads only. The total is an estimate, never an invoice.

Savings calculation: if optimizing sales aggregation brings its measured monthly cost from ₹14,400 to ₹10,800, savings are ₹3,600, a 25% reduction (3,600 ÷ 14,400). Compare equivalent periods and account for changes in data volume, run frequency, and pricing.

-- Finding 1: scheduled jobs running on all-purpose (interactive) clusters
SELECT DISTINCT t.job_id, c.cluster_name, c.cluster_source, c.owned_by
FROM system.lakeflow.job_task_run_timeline AS t
JOIN system.compute.clusters AS c ON array_contains(t.compute_ids, c.cluster_id)
WHERE c.cluster_source IN ('UI', 'API')                  -- created by hand, not by a job
  AND t.period_start_time >= date_sub(current_date(), 30);

-- Finding 2: all-purpose clusters that bill on weekends (likely idle or forgotten)
SELECT usage_metadata.cluster_id,
       identity_metadata.run_as,
       count(DISTINCT usage_date)        AS weekend_days_billed,
       round(SUM(list_cost), 2)          AS weekend_cost_usd
FROM retail_dev.cost_ops.usage_list_cost
WHERE billing_origin_product = 'ALL_PURPOSE'
  AND dayofweek(usage_date) IN (1, 7)                    -- Sunday, Saturday
  AND usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 30)
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY weekend_cost_usd DESC;

-- Finding 3: jobs whose daily cost jumped (last 7 days vs the 21 before)
WITH daily AS (
    SELECT usage_metadata.job_name AS job_name, usage_date, SUM(list_cost) AS cost
    FROM retail_dev.cost_ops.usage_list_cost
    WHERE usage_metadata.job_id IS NOT NULL
      AND usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 28)
    GROUP BY 1, 2
)
SELECT job_name,
       round(avg(CASE WHEN usage_date <  date_sub(current_date(), 7) THEN cost END), 2) AS baseline_daily_usd,
       round(avg(CASE WHEN usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 7) THEN cost END), 2) AS recent_daily_usd,
       round(avg(CASE WHEN usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 7) THEN cost END)
           / avg(CASE WHEN usage_date <  date_sub(current_date(), 7) THEN cost END), 2) AS ratio
FROM daily
GROUP BY job_name
HAVING ratio > 1.5
ORDER BY ratio DESC;

-- Finding 4: usage with no matching list price (check the join before trusting totals)
SELECT sku_name, count(*) AS records
FROM retail_dev.cost_ops.usage_list_cost
WHERE list_cost IS NULL
GROUP BY sku_name

22.8Interview Questions and Answers

Try answering each before revealing the answer.

Q1. What is a DBU?
A Databricks Unit: a normalized measure of platform consumption, not a VM hour. Consumption depends on compute size, type, and features; the price per DBU depends on the SKU, tier, region, and contract.
Q2. What makes up Azure Databricks cost?
DBUs, plus (for classic compute) Azure VMs and disks, storage, networking, and supporting services. Serverless DBU rates include the compute.
Q3. Is serverless always cheaper?
No. It removes idle time and startup overhead, which helps short or bursty work; long steady jobs may be cheaper on classic job compute. Compare measured cost per successful run.
Q4. Why are jobs on all-purpose clusters a cost problem?
All-purpose compute has a higher DBU rate than jobs compute, the cluster often stays up, and the job's cost is hidden in the shared cluster's usage instead of being attributed to the job.
Q5. How do you estimate cost from system tables?
Join system.billing.usage to system.billing.list_prices on SKU, cloud, and unit where the usage time falls within the price's validity window, and multiply quantity by the effective list price. Label it a list-price estimate.
Q6. How do you handle billing corrections?
Sum usage_quantity across all record types; retractions are negative and restatements replace them. Filtering to ORIGINAL double-counts.
Q7. How do you identify an expensive job?
Cost per job from usage_metadata.job_id, joined to run history for cost per successful run; compare against baseline, data volume, failures, and value, and look for jobs hidden on shared clusters.
Q8. Reducing cost without hurting SLAs?
Find the biggest drivers, then fix waste first (idle compute, wrong SKU, reruns, full refreshes, inefficient queries), right-size, and verify each change against runtime and SLA.
Q9. DBU consumption vs total workload cost?
DBUs measure platform usage only; total cost adds infrastructure, storage, network, and services depending on the compute model.
Q10. Allocating cost to departments?
Consistent tags (enforced by compute and budget policies), job and run_as metadata, mapping to cost centres, and a documented proxy for shared costs, separating attributed from allocated amounts.
Q11. Purpose of auto-termination?
Stops idle all-purpose clusters after a set time so they don't bill overnight or at weekends.
Q12. How do unnecessary reruns increase cost?
They repeat reads, transformations, and writes. Repair runs, idempotent tasks, and not retrying permanent errors prevent the waste.
Q13. Good cost KPIs?
Cost per successful run, cost per million records or per TB, failed-run cost, untagged share, budget utilization, and month-over-month change, measured consistently.
Q14. Why might your cost report not match the invoice?
List prices exclude discounts, commitments, and credits; currency differs; VMs, storage, and network are billed separately; and usage data arrives with a delay.
Q15. How do you attribute serverless cost to teams?
With budget (usage) policies that tag serverless usage, plus job and warehouse identifiers in usage_metadata.
